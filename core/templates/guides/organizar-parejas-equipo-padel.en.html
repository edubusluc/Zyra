Setting the pairs is the decision that matters most in a team-league padel season: two strong players who do not click lose to two more modest ones who have played together for years. This guide gives a simple method for the captain of a padel team, with the criteria that work best and how to check them with data.

1. Start with the call-up, not with the pairs

Before you look at pairs you need to know who can play. Close the call-up early, note who is out or unsure, and keep a substitute ready. A set of pairs built for ten players is useless if one drops out on Thursday. The call-up template gives you a printable format.

2. Four criteria to build a pair

  • Similar level, not identical. A balanced pair beats a very strong player next to a very weak one, because opponents always attack the weak spot.
  • Court sides. A forehand-side and a backhand-side player cover the court better than two players from the same side. If two players are only comfortable on the same side, split them.
  • Complementary styles. Someone solid in defence with someone who finishes at the net, or a pace player with a placement player.
  • Chemistry and communication. Pairs that have played together often outperform what their level suggests. It is the hardest thing to judge by eye and the one most worth measuring.

3. A five-step method

  1. List the called-up players with their level (points, ranking or your own rating).
  2. Lock in the pairs that already work: if two players keep winning together, keep them together.
  3. Fill in the rest looking for a balance of level and court sides.
  4. Check the order the pairs play in: in formats like the SNP the order is set by the points sum and the first games are worth more (see the SNP points guide).
  5. Compare with your history: has any of these pairs lost three in a row? Is there a combination you have never tried that looks good on paper?

4. Which data to look at

A few weeks of results are enough to draw useful conclusions. The statistics that really help you choose pairs are:

  • Matches and sets won per pair, not just per player.
  • Deciding sets and tie-breaks: which pairs stay firm when the match gets tight.
  • Recent streak: a pair in form deserves your trust.
  • Affinity: which partners each player wins most with.
  • Home and away: some pairs perform differently away from home.

5. Common mistakes

  • Putting the two best players together without looking at the rest: it can leave three very weak pairs.
  • Repeating the same pairs out of habit without checking they still work.
  • Changing too many pairs at once: you never learn which combination works.
  • Not recording results: without data you only have the captain's memory.
